# Sex-specific blood pressure guidance: research brief

Branch: `feature/sex-specific-guidance` · Researched 20 Nov 2026 · Status: **draft for clinician review before any public launch**

Purpose: decide what Tenang may truthfully say to women and men differently, and what it must *not* say, before building tailored content.

**Method and limits.** Web searches of guideline summaries, society statements and journal abstracts. Several findings reached me through secondary summaries (news, commentary, abstracts), not the full guideline text. Every row marked "verify" must be checked against the primary document by a clinician before launch. Nothing here is medical advice, and Tenang's tailored content stays information-only.

## 1. The big picture (what is actually different)

| # | Finding | Confidence | Source | What Tenang does with it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Blood pressure categories and treatment thresholds are the same for men and women** in the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline (it replaced 2017; the BP thresholds did not change). | High (verify against guideline text) | 2025 AHA/ACC High Blood Pressure Guideline, *Circulation* 2025;152:e114–e218 (via ACC/AHA summaries) | **No sex-specific thresholds anywhere.** Categories in the app stay as they are. Pregnancy is the one exception (row 8). |
| 2 | **Prevalence is higher in men overall, and women catch up and pass men later in life.** US adults Aug 2021–Aug 2023: 50.8% men vs 44.6% women. Ages 65–74 (NHANES 2015–18): 75.7% women vs 67.5% men. | High | CDC/NCHS data brief 2024 (via search summary); NHANES 2015–18 | "Men tend to develop high BP earlier; women's rates rise through midlife and are higher in older age." Descriptive only. |
| 3 | **Young men are less likely to know they have high BP and less likely to be treated.** Awareness in younger adults with hypertension: about 68% men vs 86% women. Suggested reason: women have more routine health contact (gynaecological, prenatal care). Men are more likely to say they have no need to see a doctor. | Medium-high (older data, 2008–2018) | *Hypertension* journal study (via search); reviews | Men's content leads with "you can't feel it; checking is the whole point", and makes a habit of measuring easy. No shaming. |
| 4 | **Menopause transition and BP: the link is real but subtle.** The AHA 2020 statement says lipid and metabolic-syndrome changes are driven more by the transition itself, while BP, insulin and glucose rises track more closely with chronological age. Limited trial data: a multidimensional lifestyle programme during the transition can prevent weight gain and lower BP. | High for the statement, moderate for the trials | AHA Scientific Statement, "Menopause Transition and Cardiovascular Disease Risk: Implications for Timing of Early Prevention", *Circulation* 2020 (doi 10.1161/CIR.0000000000000912) | Say: "BP tends to rise with age, and the menopause years are a sensible time to check regularly and look after weight, sleep, salt and movement." **Do not say menopause causes high BP.** |
| 5 | **Hot flushes and BP: mixed.** Some studies link vasomotor symptoms (VMS) to higher BP; a Baltimore cross-sectional study found no significant link after adjustment. The stronger signal is long-term: frequent or persistent VMS in SWAN were tied to a higher risk of later cardiovascular events (reported 50–77% higher). BP and heart rate rose when women woke with a hot flush (*Sleep* 2019). | Mixed / moderate | Gallicchio 2010 (*Maturitas*); SWAN analyses incl. Jackson 2016; *Sleep* 2019 (via abstracts) | Offer a **"Hot flush" note** on readings so patterns show up. Tell women frequent flushes are worth mentioning to a clinician. A measuring tip: wait until you feel settled after a flush or waking sweating. **Do not claim flushes raise BP.** |
| 6 | **Slow ("paced") breathing is NOT recommended for hot flushes.** NAMS 2023: paced respiration is Level I *not recommended* ("unlikely to provide any benefit for hot flashes"). Recommended non-hormonal options include CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, fezolinetant, oxybutynin. | High | NAMS 2023 Nonhormone Therapy Position Statement, *Menopause* 2023;30(6) | **Tenang must never say breathing treats hot flushes.** It may say slow breathing helps you feel calmer and builds a BP habit, that it has not been shown to reduce flushes, and that CBT and clinical hypnosis have good evidence and a clinician can discuss options. |
| 7 | **Menopausal hormone therapy (HT).** NAMS 2022: HT is not indicated to prevent or treat cardiovascular disease. For women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause with no contraindications, benefits generally outweigh risks for moderate-to-severe VMS; risks are higher when started later. Personalised decision, reviewed periodically. I did **not** verify HT-specific BP effects. | Medium (secondary summaries) | NAMS 2022 Hormone Therapy Position Statement (via MDedge, Healio, OHSU slides) | Say only: HT is a personal decision to discuss with a clinician, together with your BP. **No endorsement, no BP claims.** |
| 8 | **Pregnancy and BP are a separate world.** Hypertension in pregnancy is defined as 140/90 or higher; categories include chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia. AHA/ACC 2025: counsel on low-dose aspirin for people with high BP who are planning pregnancy or are pregnant; target below 140/90 in chronic hypertension in pregnancy; postpartum follow-up. | High (verify exact wording) | AHA 2021 statement "Hypertension in Pregnancy"; 2025 AHA/ACC guideline | **Tenang is not a pregnancy BP monitor.** In the pregnancy stage the app says so, shows the same-day-contact rule (140/90 or higher, or warning symptoms) and sends readings to the maternity team. No coaching. |
| 9 | **A pregnancy with hypertension raises later risk.** Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy carry a roughly 2–4 times higher risk of chronic hypertension 10–20+ years later; AHA treats adverse pregnancy outcomes as cardiovascular risk factors; postpartum year is a key time to screen. Preeclampsia HR ~1.72 and gestational hypertension HR ~1.41 for later CVD in one cohort (34-year follow-up). | High | AHA 2024 statement on the postpartum period (*Circulation*); cohort PMC9176211 | Women's guide: "Tell every clinician if you had high BP or preeclampsia in pregnancy; it is a reason to check BP regularly for life." |
| 10 | **Hormonal contraception can nudge BP.** Combined pill: average rise about 5/3 mmHg, severe in a small minority; UK MEC: combined hormonal contraception not advised for severe (≥160/100, category 4) hypertension, and also advised against for 140–159/90–99 or controlled hypertension (category 3). Progestogen-only methods seem not to raise BP. | Medium (single UK clinical reference, **UK-specific**; verify for the target country) | GPnotebook summarising UKMEC (mirror pages) | Say only: "Some hormonal contraception can raise BP a little. Have your BP checked before starting and regularly after; if yours is high, ask which methods suit you. Don't stop a method without a plan." No categories or numbers in-app. |
| 11 | **Alcohol limits differ by sex.** 2025 AHA/ACC: best to avoid; if drinking, up to 2 drinks/day men and 1 woman. Meta-analyses show benefit from cutting back in both sexes. | High | 2025 AHA/ACC guideline; AHA consumer guidance | Show the sex-based limit in the alcohol habit and guides; "less is better" for everyone. Standard drink sizes in US units; **localise for other regions.** |
| 12 | **BP medicines and sexual function (men).** Erectile dysfunction (ED) is more common in men with hypertension. Evidence that thiazides and beta-blockers contribute is conflicting; ACE inhibitors/ARBs look neutral or even positive; calcium channel blockers neutral. Reviews advise **against stopping treatment** and recommend discussing a switch. Sexual side effects are a real cause of stopping medicines. PDE-5 inhibitors are usually tolerated with BP medicines (interaction rules apply, e.g. nitrates). | Medium (reviews, some small/older) | 2025 systematic review on beta-blockers and ED (PubMed 39269577); *Vasc Health Risk Manag* review | Men's guide: "ED is common, treatable and often connected to blood vessel health. Medicines can contribute for some people. **Don't stop your medicine; ask your doctor or pharmacist about options.**" Never name specific drugs as the cause. |
| 13 | **Testosterone products can raise BP.** FDA, 28 Feb 2025: class-wide labelling now warns of increased BP; the boxed warning on cardiovascular outcomes was removed after the TRAVERSE trial. | High | FDA drug safety communication, 28 Feb 2025 | "If you use testosterone, check your BP regularly and tell the prescriber about your readings." |
| 14 | **Sleep apnoea and BP.** Obstructive sleep apnoea is very common in resistant hypertension, and more prevalent and severe in men in most cohorts (one 2025 hospital study found the opposite and suspected screening bias). Applies to both sexes. | Medium | Logan 2001; Sapiña-Beltrán et al.; 2021 *J Hypertens*; 2025 OMJ | Both guides: loud snoring, pauses in breathing, daytime sleepiness → ask about a sleep test. Men's guide puts it first. **No claims about menopause and apnoea (not verified).** |
| 15 | **ACE-inhibitor cough: sex difference unproven.** Observational reports show more cough in women, but a 2018 meta-analysis of randomised trials found no sex difference in effect. | Low/contested | VigiBase 2017; 2018 meta-analysis | **Not used.** If Medicines 101 mentions cough it is the same for everyone. |
| 16 | **Pregnancy and BP medicines.** ACE inhibitors and ARBs carry an FDA boxed warning for fetal toxicity (stop as soon as pregnancy is detected, per prescriber). | High | Current FDA labels (e.g. irbesartan, ramipril) | Women's guide + Medicines note: "If you take a BP medicine and could become pregnant or are planning a pregnancy, talk to your doctor *before* conceiving; some BP medicines are not safe in pregnancy. Don't stop on your own." |

## 2. What Tenang will NOT claim (hard rules for tailored content)

1. No sex-specific BP thresholds or targets (except the pregnancy rule in row 8, which hands off to the maternity team).
2. No "menopause causes hypertension"; no "hot flushes cause/raise BP".
3. **No claim that breathing or relaxation treats hot flushes or night sweats** (NAMS 2023: not recommended). Breathing = calm and BP habit only.
4. No recommendation for or against hormone therapy, contraception, testosterone, or any named medicine; no "stop/switch/start" instructions. Always "talk to your doctor or pharmacist".
5. No sex difference in medicine side effects unless the trial evidence supports it (so no ACE-inhibitor cough claim).
6. No statistics shown as personal risk ("you have X% chance"). Descriptive population facts only, with the source named.
7. Never assume identity: tailoring is an **optional, changeable, deletable** setting; "Not tailored" is always an option and every guide stays reachable from Learn.

## 3. Product implications

- **Tailoring is a profile, not a different app.** `tenang:profile` = `{ sex: 'female' | 'male' | 'general', stage?: ... }`, additive (no migration), included in backups, editable in Settings.
- Female stages: *periods regular · perimenopause (periods changing) · after menopause (12 months without a period) · pregnant or recently pregnant · prefer not to say.* Pregnancy shows the hand-off rule, not coaching.
- Tailored surfaces (all inside the simplified journey; no new tab): a **"For you" guide** under Learn; optional **notes** on readings (Hot flush, Alcohol); **questions for your doctor** in the doctor summary; a short **measuring tip**; a **note in Medicines 101**; for perimenopause/after menopause one extra **mood check-in** ("Hot flush").
- The choice is made **once, at first run** (an optional "Who is Tenang for?" step), saved immediately, and then kept visible by a single "For you" card on Today with a sourced daily tip. People who skipped, and existing users, see a prominent invite instead. Today's block budget is unchanged (only one of the two cards ever shows).
